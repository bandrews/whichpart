# SS14

> **Note on sources.** The part supplied here is `hongjiacheng`-branded, and that
> manufacturer's datasheet is served only through LCSC, which blocks automated
> retrieval. SS14 is an industry-standard type, so this note cites the
> JLCPCB/LCSC catalog record as `[1]` for the part actually being supplied, and
> Vishay's SS12–SS16 datasheet as `[2]` for the type — flagging where the two
> differ. See `ISSUES.md`.

## What it is

The SS14 is the workhorse Schottky rectifier for small power circuits: 1 A of
average current, 40 V of reverse voltage, in the SMA (DO-214AC) package. Its job
is to conduct with as little voltage drop as possible and to stop conducting the
instant the current reverses. [1][2]

Its most common use is as the catch diode in a non-synchronous buck converter —
the part that carries the inductor current while the switch is off. The TPS5430,
TPS54331, LM2596 and XL1509 in this catalog all need one. Vishay lists the same
uses: low-voltage high-frequency inverters, freewheeling, DC/DC converters and
polarity protection. [2]

## Key specifications

| Specification | Value | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Function | Single Schottky barrier rectifier, with a guard ring for overvoltage protection [1][2] | Standalone diode; no pairs or bridges. The guard ring is what stops a momentary overvoltage destroying it outright. |
| Repetitive peak reverse voltage | 40 V, with a 28 V RMS and 40 V DC blocking rating [1][2] | Enough for a 12 V converter with margin; not for 24 V, where the SS24 or SS34 is the right choice. |
| Average forward current | 1 A rectified. Surge: 30 A per the catalog, 40 A in Vishay's datasheet, for one 8.3 ms half sine [1][2] | The surge rating matters at startup, when the output capacitor charges. Note the branded part is rated lower than Vishay's. |
| Forward voltage | 550 mV at 1 A per the catalog; Vishay specifies 500 mV maximum at 1 A [1][2] | Roughly half a silicon diode's drop. In a 5 V converter at 1 A that is about 550 mW saved compared with a 1N4007. |
| Recovery or capacitance | No meaningful reverse-recovery charge: a Schottky conducts by majority carriers, so there is no stored minority charge to sweep out. Vishay rates the part for 10,000 V/µs of reverse dV/dt [2] | This is the property that a fast-recovery silicon diode such as the US1M cannot match, and the reason Schottkys dominate switching converters. |
| Operating temperature | Junction −55 °C to +150 °C per the catalog; Vishay specifies −65 °C to +150 °C [1][2] | Wide either way. |

## What the datasheet actually says

**Leakage rises thirtyfold between 25 °C and 100 °C.** At 25 °C the catalog's
200 µA at 40 V is right — already three orders of magnitude worse than a silicon
diode, and the fundamental Schottky trade-off. Vishay specifies the same 0.2 mA
at 25 °C but **6 mA at 100 °C**. In a buck converter that is irrelevant. In a
battery-isolation or reverse-protection circuit it is a continuous drain of a
quarter of a watt at 40 V, and it gets worse as the part self-heats — which makes
it worse still. Check this before using a Schottky as a blocking diode. [1][2]

**The thermal path is better than you might expect.** With 5 × 5 mm copper pads
Vishay gives a junction-to-ambient thermal resistance of 88 °C/W (28 °C/W
junction-to-lead). At 550 mW that is a 48 °C rise — comfortable, provided the
copper is actually there. [2]

**The forward drop falls at lower current**, which is why the SS14 stays
efficient across a converter's whole load range rather than only at full
output. [2]

## Watch out for

- **40 V is not much.** A 24 V buck converter's switch node swings to −0.5 V and
  up to 24 V plus ringing; 40 V leaves little margin. Use a higher-voltage
  Schottky.
- **Leakage matters in low-power circuits**, and it is 6 mA at 100 °C. For
  battery isolation, consider a MOSFET-based ideal diode instead. [2]
- **1 A is an average rating** that assumes the SMA package is soldered to a
  reasonable copper area. Check the dissipation: 550 mV × 1 A is 550 mW. [2]
- **Place it close to the switch node.** In a converter, the loop formed by the
  input capacitor, the switch and this diode should be as small as possible.
- **The branded part may not match Vishay's numbers exactly** — the surge and
  temperature ratings already differ. Where a figure matters, get the
  manufacturer's own datasheet from your supplier. [1]
