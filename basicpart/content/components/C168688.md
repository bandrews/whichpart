## What it is

A USB Type-C receptacle — the connector, not the electronics — with 16 contacts.
The full Type-C specification defines 24 contacts; 16-contact parts omit the
high-speed pairs that USB 3 and alternate modes need, keeping power delivery,
USB 2.0 data, the two CC pins and the sideband pins. [1]

For a board that only needs USB 2.0 and power — which is most microcontroller
boards — a 16-contact receptacle is the right choice: fewer pins to route, fewer
to solder, and the same physical connector the user plugs into. [1]

## What can be said

**The footprint is the whole risk, and the repository already warns about it.**
The curated record's note reads: "Ordinary Extended part; verify its exact
footprint and JLCPCB assembly restrictions before use." That is exactly right.
USB-C receptacles vary in shell tab position, board cut-out, and whether the
part is top-mount, mid-mount or through-hole hybrid. A wrong footprint is not a
routing error you can patch — the connector physically does not fit. [1]

**Do not forget the CC resistors.** A USB-C device that wants to draw power must
present 5.1 kΩ pull-downs on CC1 and CC2 (two separate resistors, not one). This
is the single most common USB-C design mistake, and no connector does it for you.

**Assembly restrictions matter for connectors specifically.** JLCPCB restricts
some connectors from automated assembly on some services. That is worth checking
before the design is committed, not after.

## Watch out for

- **Verify the footprint against the manufacturer's drawing.** Not against
  another board's library.
- **Fit two 5.1 kΩ CC pull-down resistors** on a device-side port.
- **Extended-tier assembly costs more** and may be restricted.
- **No specification data is available in this repository.** Get the real
  datasheet before committing.
