# TJA1050T/CM,118

## What it is

Like the SN65HVD230, the TJA1050 is the physical-layer chip between a CAN
controller and the two-wire bus. The difference is that it runs from 5 V rather
than 3.3 V — but with input levels that accept 3.3 V logic, so it still works
directly with a modern microcontroller. [1]

Its design emphasis is electromagnetic behaviour. NXP (then Philips) built it to
replace the PCA82C250 with much lower emissions, achieved by carefully matching
the CANH and CANL output waveforms. [1]

## Key specifications

| Specification | Value | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Function | High-speed CAN transceiver with differential transmit and receive, a silent mode that disables the transmitter, and a TXD dominant time-out [1] | The TXD time-out stops a stuck-low transmit line from jamming the whole bus — a genuinely valuable failure mode to have covered. |
| Signalling standard | Fully compatible with ISO 11898 [1] | Interoperates with any compliant CAN node. |
| Maximum data rate | Up to 1 Mbaud [1] | Classic CAN's ceiling; not CAN FD. |
| Supply voltage | 4.75 V to 5.25 V [1] | A 5 V part, but with input levels compatible with 3.3 V and 5 V devices. |
| Isolation or protection | Bus pins withstand −27 V to +40 V DC with no time limit; short-circuit proof to battery and to ground; thermal protection; bus pins protected against automotive transients; an unpowered node does not disturb the bus [1] | The −27 V to +40 V range is an automotive specification, and it is the reason this part appears in vehicles. |
| Operating temperature | Virtual junction temperature −40 °C to +150 °C [1] | Specified as a junction limit. |

## What the datasheet actually says

**At least 110 nodes can be connected** — a figure of the same order as the
SN65HVD230's 120, and far more than the 32-node RS-485 convention. High-impedance
receivers are what make node counts like this possible. [1]

**There is no standby or sleep mode.** NXP says so explicitly in the list of
differences from the PCA82C250 this part replaced: silent mode disables the
transmitter, but nothing powers the device down. Supply current is 50 mA typical
and 75 mA maximum while the bus is driven dominant, and 5 mA typical, 10 mA
maximum when recessive. On a battery-powered node, budget for that or switch the
transceiver's supply externally. [1]

**Propagation delay from TXD to RXD is 250 ns maximum.** In CAN, loop delay
directly limits how long the bus can be at a given bit rate, so this number
matters for network design. [1]

**Silent mode disables the transmitter** while leaving the receiver working —
useful for listening to a bus without any possibility of disturbing it, for
diagnostics or for a node that has detected its own fault. [1]

**Thermal protection switches off the transmitter** if the junction temperature
gets too high, rather than letting the part fail. [1]

**An unpowered node does not disturb the bus lines.** This was one of the
improvements over the earlier PCA82C250, and it matters on a bus where individual
nodes may be switched off. [1]

## Watch out for

- **5 V supply required**, even though the logic inputs accept 3.3 V. You need a
  5 V rail somewhere.
- **Termination is your job.** 120 Ω at each far end of the bus.
- **You still need a CAN controller** in your microcontroller.
- **Not CAN FD.** For the faster data phase, use a transceiver rated for it.
- **The datasheet is from 2003.** The part remains widely used; it is simply a
  mature design.

## In this catalog

Preferred Extended part in SOP-8. At the 2026-07-24 snapshot: 190,927 in stock,

4.75 V–5.25 V supply, 1 Mbps, TXD dominant timeout, silent mode, thermal
