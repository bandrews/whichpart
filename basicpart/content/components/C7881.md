## What it is

A charge pump — sometimes called a switched-capacitor converter — moves charge
between capacitors to produce a voltage the input does not have. The ICL7660A's
usual job is inversion: give it +5 V and it produces −5 V, using two external
capacitors and no inductor at all. [1]

That matters whenever a mostly-digital board needs a small negative rail: for an
op-amp that must swing below ground, for an LCD bias, or for a sensor bridge. The
alternative — a switching converter with an inductor — is bigger, noisier and
harder to lay out. [1]

## Key specifications

| Specification | Value | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Output voltage | −1.5 V to −12 V, mirroring the input [1] | The output magnitude follows the input; it is an inverter, not a regulator. |
| Output current | Limited by the 97 Ω output resistance [1] | **This is the number that decides everything.** At 10 mA you lose about 1 V; at 50 mA nearly 5 V. Charge pumps are for milliamps, not hundreds of milliamps. |
| Input voltage range | 1.5 V to 12 V [1] | Wide, covering everything from a lithium cell to a 12 V rail. |
| Switching frequency | 8 kHz, with adaptive switching frequency listed as a feature [1] | Low. That keeps quiescent current down, but it means the output ripple is at an audible frequency and the capacitors must be large. |
| Efficiency | Up to 98 % [1] | Excellent — but that figure is at light load, where the 97 Ω output resistance costs little. |
| Operating temperature | −40 °C to +85 °C [1] | Industrial range. |

## What the specification implies

**97 Ω of output resistance is the whole design constraint.** The output voltage
is the input voltage minus the load current times that resistance. Draw 20 mA
from a −5 V rail and you actually get about −3 V. Size your expectations from
Ohm's law, not from the input voltage.

**Quiescent current is 100 µA.** Low enough to leave running in a
battery-powered device, especially given the alternative is an inductor-based
converter.

**Cascading is supported.** The catalog lists it as a feature: two devices in
series can double the voltage, so +5 V in can give −10 V out, at the cost of
twice the output resistance.

**8 kHz is audible.** Ceramic capacitors are piezoelectric; a charge pump
switching at 8 kHz with ceramic capacitors can produce a faint whine. If the
product is used in a quiet room, that is worth checking.

## Watch out for

- **Milliamps, not hundreds of milliamps.** The 97 Ω output resistance is not
  negotiable.
- **It does not regulate.** The output tracks the input and sags with load. If
  you need a stable negative rail, follow it with a small LDO.
- **Capacitor choice matters.** The pump capacitor and the reservoir capacitor
  both affect output resistance and ripple; use the datasheet's values.
- **12 V input maximum.**
