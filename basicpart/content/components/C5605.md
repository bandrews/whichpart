# 74HC14D,653

## What it is

A Schmitt trigger is a logic input with two different switching thresholds: it
takes a higher voltage to make it flip one way than the other. That hysteresis
is what turns a slow, noisy, or bouncing signal into a clean square edge. The
74HC14 packs six of them, each followed by an inverter, into a 14-pin SOIC. [1]

You reach for it to clean up a mechanical switch, square off a sensor output
that changes gradually, buffer a long cable run, or build a simple relaxation
oscillator from one gate, a resistor and a capacitor. [1]

## What the datasheet actually says

**"Unlimited input rise and fall times."** Nexperia states this explicitly, and it
is the entire point of a Schmitt trigger. An ordinary logic gate fed a slowly
rising edge will oscillate as it crosses its threshold; this part will not. [1]

**Input clamp diodes enable over-voltage interfacing.** The datasheet says the
clamp diodes let you use current-limiting resistors to interface inputs to
voltages in excess of V<sub>CC</sub>. That is a documented technique, not a
hack — though the resistor is mandatory. [1]

**It is listed under "astable and monostable multivibrators" in the
applications.** The single-gate RC oscillator is a supported use, not folklore. [1]

**Latch-up performance exceeds 100 mA per JESD 78 Class II Level B**, and ESD
protection is HBM class 2 (over 2,000 V) and CDM class C3 (over 1,000 V). [1]

## Watch out for

- **HC is not HCT.** If you are feeding it 3.3 V logic from a 5 V supply, you
  want the 74HCT14 instead — its input threshold is TTL-referenced.
- **The output is inverting.** Six inverters, not six buffers. If you need
  non-inverting Schmitt buffers, that is the 74HC17.
- **Tie unused inputs to a rail.** Floating CMOS inputs oscillate and waste
  power.
- **Delay at 2 V is 41 ns typical**, against 15 ns at 4.5 V. Do not assume the
  4.5 V timing at low supply voltages. [1]
