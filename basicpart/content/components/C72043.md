# 19-217/GHC-YR1S2/3T

## What it is

A green indicator LED in the 0603 package — 1.6 × 0.8 mm, the size of a grain of
coarse salt. Everlight calls the chip "brilliant green" and puts it behind a
water-clear lens, so the part looks colourless when it is off and vivid green
when it is on. Green is the colour the human eye is most sensitive to, which is
why a green indicator looks brighter than a red or blue one of the same measured
intensity. [1]

The chip is indium gallium nitride, the same family as blue and white LEDs, and
that is the thing to know before you design it in. A red LED drops roughly 2 V;
this one is specified at 3.30 V typical. On a 3.3 V rail there is almost nothing
left over for the resistor that sets the current — see below. [1]

One caution about sources. Everlight has published two different datasheets for
this exact part number, and they do not agree. The figures here come from the
current one, DSE-0006617 Rev. 4 of May 2017. The older SZDSE-197-G02 Rev. 2 of
April 2009 is still widely mirrored by distributors and gives a different peak
wavelength and a different test condition for forward voltage. [1][4]

## What the datasheet actually says

**3.30 V is measured at 20 mA, and you will not run it there.** In Rev. 4 the
condition column brackets the whole optical block — intensity, wavelengths and
forward voltage alike — at `IF=20mA`. Everlight's own Forward Current vs.
Forward Voltage curve shows conduction starting a little under 2.8 V and
reaching 20 mA at roughly 3.3 V, so at the 1–3 mA an indicator actually needs,
the typical drop is nearer 2.8 V than 3.3 V. That is the difference between "a
3.3 V rail cannot drive this LED" and "a 3.3 V rail can drive it, with about
half a volt across the resistor". Half a volt is not much: part-to-part spread
in forward voltage then swamps the resistor, and brightness varies visibly
between units. A 5 V rail, or a constant-current driver, makes the problem go
away. [1]

**The two Everlight datasheets disagree on real numbers.** The 2009 sheet gives
a peak wavelength of 525 nm and specifies forward voltage at 5 mA; the 2017
sheet gives 518 nm and specifies it at 20 mA. The forward-voltage limits
themselves are unchanged at 2.70/3.30/3.70 V, which means the *same* numbers now
carry a four-times-higher test current — a real tightening, not a re-typing.
LCSC's own product description says "Emerald Green 518nm", so the catalog is
following the current sheet. [1][2][4]

**The order code names the bins, and they cover everything Everlight makes.** In
`19-217/GHC-YR1S2/3T`, `Y` is the dominant-wavelength bin (525.0–530.0 nm) and
`R1S2` names the lowest and highest luminous-intensity bins: R1 is 112–140 mcd,
R2 is 140–180, S1 is 180–225 and S2 is 225–285. Because R1 to S2 spans every bin
for this device, you are not buying a brightness match — two parts from the same
order can differ 2.5:1. Everlight prints the actual bin on the reel label as
`CAT` (intensity) and `HUE` (wavelength), so you can find out what you got, but
you cannot specify it in the order code. [1]

**Brightness barely moves with temperature, but the current rating does.** The
relative-intensity-versus-temperature curve is close to flat from −40 °C to
+100 °C — an advantage of green InGaN over red chemistry, which visibly dims
when warm. The derating curve is the opposite story: 25 mA holds only to about
25 °C ambient, then falls roughly linearly to about 5 mA at 85 °C. An indicator
is visible at a few milliamps, so this rarely bites, but it is a reason not to
design to the current rating. [1]

## Watch out for

- **Check your rail before anything else.** 3.3 V leaves roughly half a volt
  across the series resistor at indicator currents, and less on a high-voltage
  part. 5 V is far more predictable. [1]
- **Always fit a series resistor.** Everlight's own precautions put this first:
  without one, a small shift in voltage causes a large change in current and the
  LED burns out. [1]
- **Reverse voltage is only 5 V**, and that is an absolute maximum, not a rating
  for continuous reverse operation. [1]
- **150 V ESD, human body model.** Very low by modern standards — many parts in
  this catalog withstand 2,000 V. Treat these as static-sensitive on the bench
  and in assembly. [1]
- **Comparisons with the blue LED (C72041) need care.** Its intensity is
  specified at 5 mA, this one's at 20 mA. The bare numbers — 28.5 against
  285 mcd — are not measured under the same conditions. [1]
- **Extended-tier assembly costs more** than the Basic-tier red (C2286) and
  white (C2290) LEDs sitting beside it in the same recommendation group. [3]
- **Polarity.** Check the cathode mark on Everlight's package drawing against
  your footprint — 0603 LED polarity marks are not standardised across vendors. [1]
- **Assembly constraints.** Reflow at 260 °C for at most 10 s and no more than
  two passes; hand soldering at 350 °C for 3 s with an iron of 25 W or less. The
  part is moisture-sensitive, with a one-year floor life once the foil bag is
  opened. [1]
