## What it is

Segment LCDs — the kind in a multimeter, a thermostat or a bathroom scale — are
awkward to drive directly. They need alternating waveforms on every segment (a
steady DC voltage destroys them), multiplexed across several common lines, and
that is a continuous real-time job. The HT1621B does all of it. [1]

You write display data into its RAM over a three-wire serial interface and it
generates the waveforms forever afterwards, with no further attention from your
microcontroller. [1]

## Key specifications

| Specification | Value | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Function | Segment LCD controller/driver with display RAM, 32 segments × 4 commons [1] | 128 individually controllable segments — enough for a multi-digit numeric display with annunciators. |
| Signalling standard | Three-wire serial interface [1] | Three microcontroller pins for the whole display. |
| Maximum data rate | Internal oscillator 256 kHz [1] | The interface speed is not the constraint; the display refresh is handled internally. |
| Supply voltage | 2.4 V to 5.2 V [1] | Works on 3 V coin cells and on 5 V logic alike. |
| Isolation or protection | None | — |
| Operating temperature | −40 °C to +85 °C [1] | Industrial range. |

## What the specification implies

**Duty and bias are configurable:** 1/4, 1/3 or 1/2 duty, and 1/3 or 1/2 bias.
These must match the LCD glass you are using — the glass's datasheet states what
it expects, and getting it wrong gives you ghosting on segments that should be
off, or poor contrast on segments that should be on. [1]

**Supply current is 60 µA.** For a battery instrument that leaves the display on
permanently, that is the number that matters, and it is low enough that the LCD
itself is essentially free to run. [1]

**Display RAM means you write once.** Unlike bit-banging an LCD directly, you
update only the digits that change and the driver keeps refreshing the rest. That
is what frees the microcontroller to sleep.

**It drives glass, not a module.** You need a bare segment LCD and a matching
footprint or connector — usually a zebra strip or heat-seal tail. That mechanical
work is often more effort than the electronics.

## Watch out for

- **Match duty and bias to your glass.** This is the most common source of a
  disappointing display.
- **Never apply DC to an LCD segment.** The driver handles this, but a stuck
  output or a wiring error will permanently damage the glass.
- **48 pins for 36 signals** — most of the package is display connections, and
  routing them is the bulk of the layout work.
- **Sourcing the glass is the hard part.** The driver is easy; a matching custom
  LCD is a lead-time item.
